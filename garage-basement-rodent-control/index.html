Getting started.

Most garage and basement calls come in after someone finally moves a stack of boxes that has not been touched in months and finds droppings, a chewed corner, or a nest tucked behind storage. Others notice a smell before they see anything at all, especially in a garage that shares a wall with living space. Either way, describing what was found and roughly how long it has been since the area was last checked helps the technician plan the visit before arriving, since a garage that has not been disturbed in a year is treated differently than one checked weekly.

Why garages draw activity.

A garage is often the least disturbed part of a house, especially one used mainly for storage rather than daily parking, and that quiet makes it appealing to rodents looking for a place to nest undisturbed. Cardboard boxes, stored fabric, and insulation in an attached garage's ceiling all make good nesting material, and a garage door that does not seal tightly along the bottom is one of the most common entry points on any property, old or new.

Detached garages near the Historic Downtown District, some of which predate the house they sit next to, tend to have more gaps around aging doors and foundations than an attached garage built to current code. Either way, the fix starts the same way, tracing where the animals are actually getting in rather than assuming the door alone is the problem.

Commercial and shared-wall garages.

Some properties near downtown share a garage or storage structure between units, and a rodent problem in a shared space affects everyone using it, not just the person who happened to notice it first. For a multi-unit property or a small business with an attached storage garage, we recommend the same combination of removal and sealing used on a single-family home, since the underlying entry points and travel routes work the same way regardless of who owns the building. Businesses that need ongoing documentation of this kind of work should look at Commercial Rodent Control for a monitoring schedule rather than a single visit.

What gets checked during a garage visit.

A technician checks the door seal along the full width, the gap where a garage foundation meets the slab, any vents or utility penetrations, and stored items for gnaw marks or droppings. Basements and lower-level storage areas in homes that have them get the same treatment, with particular attention to where plumbing or electrical lines pass through a foundation wall.

Protecting what is stored there.

Once activity is confirmed, Rodent Trapping and Removal clears out what is already present while Rodent Exclusion and Entry Point Sealing closes the door gap, vent, or foundation crack that let them in. Stored boxes with any sign of gnawing or droppings are pointed out during the visit so you know what may need to be discarded rather than kept.

Wiring and electrical concerns.

Garages often have exposed wiring for a garage door opener, outdoor lighting, or an electric vehicle charger, and rodents gnawing on that wiring create both a fire risk and a repeat repair bill. If gnawed wiring is found, we flag it clearly and recommend having it checked by an electrician before use, since chewed insulation on a live circuit is not something to leave for later.

A quiet garage is exactly what a rodent is looking for.

Seasonal patterns for garages in Mount Dora.

Garage activity tends to follow the same broader seasonal pattern as the rest of the house, picking up when cooler weather from late fall into winter sends rodents looking for shelter. A second, smaller rise comes during the wettest stretch of summer, when heavy rain pushes ground-level activity toward higher, drier structures. A garage that backs onto a wooded lot or sits close to drainage tends to see this more than one set farther from any green space.

Attached versus detached garages.

An attached garage shares at least one wall and often a ceiling cavity with the living space of the house, which means an unresolved rodent problem there is closer to becoming an indoor problem than one in a detached structure across the yard. We treat attached garages with the same attention given to the interior of the house itself, since the shared wall or attic space is effectively part of the same building envelope.

After the visit.

A follow-up check confirms the door seal and any other sealed points are holding, and that no new droppings have appeared among stored items. If a basement or crawl space beneath the garage was also treated, that gets checked separately, since moisture and access differ enough from the garage itself to matter.

Before we arrive.

  • Keep children and pets away from any area with droppings, a nest, or a suspected entry point.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings; that can spread contamination into the air.
  • Do not set out your own loose bait or poison before the visit.
  • Note when and where you have seen or heard activity so the technician can start there.

Questions about garage and basement rodent control

Why is my garage worse than the rest of the house?

Garages are often the least disturbed space in a home, which makes them appealing for undisturbed nesting, especially when used mainly for storage.

Can rodents damage a garage door opener?

Yes, gnawed wiring near the motor is a real fire and repair risk, and we flag it clearly if found during a visit.

Do basements get the same treatment as garages?

Yes, with attention to the specific access points a basement has, such as utility penetrations through the foundation.

What should I do with gnawed storage boxes?

Items with gnaw marks or droppings are worth discarding rather than keeping, since they may carry contamination and can hold nesting material.

How do I know if my garage door is a problem?

A worn or gapped seal along the bottom of the door is one of the most common entry points, and it is checked as part of any garage visit.
