What the inspection covers.

An inspection starts at the attic access point and works outward, checking rafters, insulation, and any wiring for droppings, grease marks, or gnaw damage. From there the technician moves to ground level, checking the foundation perimeter, vents, utility penetrations, and any gap around a garage door or crawl space access. In Mount Dora's older homes near the Historic Downtown District, we pay particular attention to where an addition meets the original structure, since that seam is a common weak point.

Outdoor areas get the same attention as the interior. Fence lines, sheds, woodpiles, and dense landscaping are checked for burrow openings and travel routes, especially on lots near Palm Island Park or the lakefront where tree cover is heavier. We are looking for the full picture, not just the room where the noise was loudest.

Documenting what we find.

Every entry point, dropping concentration, and travel route gets noted during the walkthrough, and we point things out as we go rather than saving everything for a summary at the end. If there are multiple entry points, which is common in a house with more than one roofline, we explain how each one fits into the overall plan rather than treating them as separate, unrelated problems.

How the estimate works.

The estimate breaks down labor and material separately where it matters, such as when a job includes both sealing work and insulation replacement, so you can see what portion of the cost covers each part. If a homeowner wants to handle part of the work themselves, such as clearing stored items from an attic before a technician arrives, we say so clearly rather than bundling every possible task into a single line item.

Once the inspection is finished, we walk through what was found, what needs to happen, and what it costs before any work begins. There is no pressure to decide on the spot, and the estimate covers the specific work identified on your property rather than a generic package. If the scope changes once work starts, such as finding a second entry point behind drywall, we explain that before proceeding rather than after.

Every estimate is written in plain language rather than trade shorthand, so you know exactly what sealing material is going where and why a particular area needs attention before another one does.

Mice, rats, or something else.

Squirrels, raccoons, and even large insects can produce sounds or damage that get mistaken for a rodent problem at first, especially for someone who has never dealt with any wildlife issue before. Confirming the actual animal before quoting a rodent-specific treatment plan avoids the wasted expense of treating for the wrong species entirely, which is a real risk when a homeowner tries to diagnose the problem alone from sound and guesswork.

Not every scratching sound turns out to be a rodent. Part of the inspection is confirming what is actually present, since droppings, gnaw patterns, and nesting material differ between Mouse Control and Rat Control situations, and the treatment plan depends on getting that right from the start.

Why an inspection matters before spending on treatment.

Treating a symptom without finding the source is a common way homeowners end up paying for the same problem twice. A homeowner who sets store-bought traps in a kitchen without knowing rats are actually entering through a gap at the roofline will keep seeing activity no matter how many traps get set, since the traps were never near the real travel route. An inspection exists to prevent that kind of wasted effort by identifying the actual path before any treatment plan gets built.

Some homeowners schedule an inspection purely as a precaution before buying a home or ahead of a planned renovation, even with no current signs of activity. That kind of preventive inspection follows the same process, checking the attic, foundation, and yard for anything that might become a problem later, and it gives a buyer or homeowner a clear picture of the property before committing time or money to other work.

What happens after.

If you move forward with treatment, the inspection findings become the plan for that visit and any follow-up. If you decide to wait, you still have a clear, written picture of what was found and what it would take to address it, with nothing left vague or unexplained.

Questions about rodent inspection and estimates

Do I need an inspection if I already know I have mice?

Not always. If activity is obvious and recent, we can often go straight to trapping and sealing on the same visit.

How long does an inspection take?

Most inspections take under an hour, longer if the property has multiple structures or a large yard to check.

Is the estimate written down?

Yes. You get a clear explanation of what was found and what the work costs before anything starts.

What if you find something unexpected once work starts?

We explain any change in scope, such as a second entry point behind drywall, before proceeding rather than after.

Can you tell mice and rats apart just by looking?

Droppings, gnaw patterns, and nesting material differ enough between species that a technician can usually confirm which one is present during the inspection.
