Starting a trapping job.

Most calls start with a description of what has been seen or heard, whether that is a mouse spotted crossing a kitchen floor at night, droppings found in a pantry, or scratching coming from an attic access panel above a hallway. That description, along with roughly how long the activity has been going on, gives the technician a starting point for where to concentrate the first round of traps before the visit even begins. Homes with a recent addition or a roof replaced at a different time than the rest of the structure often have their own particular travel routes worth mentioning up front.

Why placement matters more than trap count.

Setting a dozen traps in the wrong spots does less than setting three in the right ones. Mice and rats travel along edges, following walls, pipes, and rafters rather than crossing open floor space, so a trap in the middle of a garage floor usually catches nothing while one tucked against the baseboard six feet away catches something the first night. A technician reads the droppings, grease marks, and gnaw damage from the inspection and places traps directly on that route.

Rodent Inspection and Estimates is where that travel route gets mapped in detail if you want the full picture first, but for an active, obvious problem we often go straight into trapping on the same visit, adjusting placement as the first day's results come in.

Snap traps, bait stations and when each is used.

Indoor placements near food storage or in a wall void typically use enclosed bait stations or covered snap traps that keep pets and children away from the mechanism. Attic and crawl space placements, where nothing else has access, can use open snap traps for faster results. Outdoor placements along fence lines and near burrow entrances almost always use secured, tamper-resistant stations.

Checking and adjusting the plan.

Traps are checked on a schedule rather than left indefinitely. A placement that is not producing results after a few days gets moved rather than left in a spot that clearly is not on the animal's path. A placement that is working hard gets a second trap added nearby, since heavy activity in one spot usually means more than one animal using that route.

This is where the inspection pays off during the removal phase. Knowing that a rat problem traces to a gap at the roofline, for example, means attic traps go near that gap first rather than being spread evenly across the whole space.

Mount Dora properties with more ground to cover.

Larger lots near Lakeside or properties backing onto green space around the lake often need outdoor placements in addition to anything found inside, since a Norway rat population working a yard will keep pressuring the house even after an indoor problem is cleared. We treat indoor and outdoor activity as connected rather than as two separate jobs when the property calls for it.

What removal alone does not fix.

Trapping clears out the animals that are already inside, but it does not close the opening they used to get there. Rodent Exclusion and Entry Point Sealing is the other half of most jobs, and we recommend both together whenever an active entry point was found during inspection, since trapping without sealing usually means new activity within weeks.

Clearing the animals already inside is only half the job.

Humane and low-stress handling.

Traps are checked promptly rather than left for extended periods, and removal is handled quickly once an animal is caught. Dead animal disposal is included as part of the same visit, so you are not left to deal with that part yourself.

Timing removal around Mount Dora's seasons.

Removal calls follow a similar pattern to the rest of the network in this climate. Cooler overnight temperatures from late fall into winter push more animals toward warm attics and wall cavities, while the wettest weeks of summer flood low-lying ground near drainage areas and lakeshore edges, pushing Norway rats and mice toward higher, drier ground closer to a house. Neither pattern means the other season is quiet, since a food source in a garage or pantry can sustain activity year round regardless of the weather outside.

After the activity stops.

A final check confirms traps are clear across two or more visits before we call a job finished. If the property has an outdoor component, we also check that burrow openings have not reopened, since ground-level activity can restart from a nearby yard even after the immediate area is cleared. We would rather schedule one more short visit than leave the outcome uncertain.

Before we arrive.

  • Keep children and pets away from any area with droppings, a nest, or a suspected entry point.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings; that can spread contamination into the air.
  • Do not set out your own loose bait or poison before the visit.
  • Note when and where you have seen or heard activity so the technician can start there.

Questions about rodent trapping and removal

How many traps does a typical job need?

It depends on the property and how many entry points and travel routes are found. A small, contained problem might need only a few placements, while a larger lot with outdoor activity needs more.

How often are traps checked?

On a regular schedule until activity stops, with placements adjusted along the way if a spot is not producing results.

Do you remove the dead animals?

Yes, disposal is included as part of the visit.

Is trapping enough on its own?

Trapping clears out what is already inside, but sealing the entry point is what keeps new animals from following the same path in.

Can you trap outdoors if the activity is in the yard?

Yes, secured outdoor stations are used along fence lines, sheds, and burrow entrances when a property has ground-level activity.
